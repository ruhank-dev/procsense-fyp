NG253 1.8.2 For people aged 16 or over who are at high risk of severe illness or death from sepsis: arrange for a clinician with core competencies in the care of acutely ill patients (FY2 level or above) to urgently assess the person's condition
NG253 1.8.3 Give people aged 16 or over who are at high risk of severe illness or death from sepsis broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotic treatment, within 1 hour of calculating the person's NEWS2 score on initial assessment in the emergency department or on ward deterioration.
NG253 (medium risk) arrange for a clinician with core competencies in the care of acutely ill patients (FY2 level or above) to review the person's condition and venous lactate results within 1 hour of the person being assessed as at medium risk.
NG253 1.8.17 For people at medium risk of severe illness or death from sepsis, a clinician ... should consider: deferring administration of a broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment for up to 3 hours after calculating the person's first NEWS2 score
NG253 (low risk) ... deferring administration of a broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment for up to 6 hours after calculating the person's first NEWS2 score
NG253 1.8.22 For people aged 16 or over at low risk of severe illness or death from sepsis: arrange for registered health practitioner review within 1 hour of the person being assessed as at low risk
NG253 1.8.4 Give an intravenous fluid bolus without delay (within 1 hour of identifying that they are at high risk) ... 1.8.6 Give an initial bolus of 250 ml. Ideally, give this over 10 to 15 minutes.
NG89 1.3.13 Advise people to consider stopping oestrogen-containing oral contraceptives or hormone replacement therapy 4 weeks before elective surgery.
NG89 1.14.3 Add pharmacological VTE prophylaxis for a minimum of 7 days for people undergoing abdominal surgery whose risk of VTE outweighs their risk of bleeding
NG89 risk assessment tool, thrombosis risk: Age > 60; Obesity (BMI >30 kg/m2); Total anaesthetic + surgical time > 90 minutes; Surgery involving pelvis or lower limb with a total anaesthetic + surgical time > 60 minutes.
NG89 risk assessment tool, bleeding risk: Thrombocytopaenia (platelets< 75x109/l); Concurrent use of anticoagulants known to increase the risk of bleeding (such as warfarin with INR >2).
NICE NG125 1.2.13 Do not use antibiotic prophylaxis routinely for clean non-prosthetic uncomplicated surgery.
NICE CG188 1.2.4 Offer early laparoscopic cholecystectomy (to be carried out within 1 week of diagnosis) to people with acute cholecystitis.
NICE CG174 1.3.1 If patients need IV fluid resuscitation, use crystalloids that contain sodium in the range 130 to 154 mmol/l, with a bolus of 500 ml over less than 15 minutes.
NICE NG148 criteria for acute kidney injury include a fall in urine output to less than 0.5 ml/kg/hour.
NICE NG243 1.4.8 For people having planned or emergency surgery or invasive medical procedures, offer glucocorticoids (intramuscular or intravenous) in accordance with tables 1 and 2 in Woodcock et al.

CPOC Guideline for Perioperative Care for People with Diabetes Mellitus Undergoing Elective and Emergency Surgery (2023, PDF downloaded from cpoc.org.uk and read), Practical Resource 1.2:
Metformin: take as normal the day before; if taken once or twice a day take as normal on the day; if three times a day omit the lunchtime dose. Sulphonylurea: omit on the morning of surgery. DPP4 inhibitor: take as normal. GLP-1 receptor agonist: take as normal (CPOC expert committee: continue, with precautions to avoid pulmonary aspiration; the ASA advises withholding). SGLT-2 inhibitors: omit on the day before surgery and omit on the day of surgery. Measure capillary blood ketones daily if normally on an SGLT2 inhibitor even if glucose is normal. Use a variable rate intravenous insulin infusion if fasting with more than one missed meal; dose of long-acting insulin should be 80%.

UKCPA Handbook of Perioperative Medicines (https://periop-handbook.ukclinicalpharmacy.org/, pages read 10 October 2026):
Warfarin: if INR 2.0-3.0 discontinue 5 days prior; this should allow the INR to fall to 1.4 by the day of operation; check INR on admission.
Dabigatran, low/moderate bleeding risk surgery: CrCl 50 or more omit day -1 and morning of surgery (3 doses); CrCl 30-49 omit from day -2 (5 doses); high bleeding risk CrCl 50 or more omit from day -2; CrCl 30-49 omit from day -4.
Aspirin, non-cardiac surgery: low dose (150 mg or less) continue, except high bleeding risk procedures where stopping 7 days pre-operatively is considered; as analgesia consider stopping 7 days pre-operatively.
Clopidogrel: for other than minor surgery stop 7 days pre-operatively; if it cannot be continued and thrombosis risk is high consider shortening to 5 days.
Beta-blockers (bisoprolol): continue; sudden withdrawal should be avoided, especially in ischaemic heart disease. Atorvastatin and amlodipine: continue. Furosemide and bendroflumethiazide: continue; correct electrolyte abnormalities.
Ramipril: omit on the morning of surgery as a practical approach, although some centres omit up to 48 hours pre-operatively.
Antiepileptics overview: there is usually no clinical reason for epilepsy patients undergoing surgery to withdraw from their medication; abrupt withdrawal should be avoided.
Herbal remedies position statement: for elective surgery stop all herbal medicines one or two weeks pre-operatively.
Ibuprofen: check blood pressure and renal function pre-operatively; for high bleeding risk procedures weigh stopping 1 day before.
UK Kidney Association Clinical Practice Guideline Management of Hyperkalaemia in Adults, updated July 2026 (PDF read): hyperkalaemia mild 5.5-5.9, moderate 6.0-6.4, severe 6.5 or more mmol/l; with ECG changes give 30 ml 10% calcium gluconate over 10 minutes (changed from 15 minutes in 2026) or 10 ml 10% calcium chloride over 5 minutes; insulin-glucose (10 units soluble insulin in 25 g glucose); then 10% glucose 50 ml/hour for 5 hours if pre-treatment blood glucose is below 7 mmol/l.

Electronic Medicines Compendium SmPCs (read 10 October 2026): Ondansetron 2mg/ml solution for injection (product 11553): contraindicated with apomorphine; prolongs the QT interval in a dose-dependent manner. Metoclopramide 5 mg/ml injection (product 6283): contraindicated in gastrointestinal haemorrhage, mechanical obstruction or perforation; special care with QT prolongation. Ticagrelor (Brilique, product 5767): discontinue 5 days prior to elective surgery if antiplatelet effect is not desired. Prasugrel (Efient, product 100009): discontinue at least 7 days prior to elective surgery. Naloxone (product 6344): reversal of respiratory depression caused by opioids.
BSACI guideline, Management of allergy to penicillins and other beta-lactams (Mirakian et al., Clin Exp Allergy 2015;45:300-327, PDF read): reactions are classed as immediate (IgE-mediated, within minutes to 1 hour) or non-immediate (more than 60 minutes to days); cross-reactivity depends on side-chain homology; if a cephalosporin is needed in a patient with a history of penicillin allergy and positive tests, use one with a different side chain after testing; patch tests are used for severe cutaneous reactions such as SJS/TEN, DRESS and AGEP.

Tokyo Guidelines acute cholecystitis (open-access texts read 10 October 2026: the TG07 paper, Hirota et al., J Hepatobiliary Pancreat Surg 2007, PMC2784516, and a 2015 paper reproducing the TG13 criteria, PMC4602922; the TG18 meeting records on jshbps.jp cover antimicrobial questions, not these criteria):
Diagnosis: A local inflammatory signs (Murphy sign, right upper quadrant mass, pain or tenderness); B systemic signs (fever, raised CRP, raised white cell count); C imaging; one positive in A and one in B; C confirms when inflammatory signs are insufficient.
Grade III: cardiovascular (hypotension needing dopamine 5 or more or any dobutamine), neurological, respiratory (PaO2/FiO2 below 300), renal (oliguria, creatinine above 2.0 mg/dl), hepatic (PT-INR above 1.5), haematological (platelets below 100,000/mm3). Grade II: white count above 18,000, palpable tender mass, symptoms over 72 hours, marked local inflammation. Grade I: neither.
WSES Jerusalem guidelines for acute appendicitis, 2020 update (Di Saverio et al., World J Emerg Surg 2020;15:27, PDF read): Recommendation 7.1 a single preoperative dose of broad-spectrum antibiotics (from 0 to 60 minutes before skin incision), and against postoperative antibiotics for uncomplicated appendicitis; Recommendation 7.2 against prolonging antibiotics beyond 3 to 5 days for complicated appendicitis with adequate source control; Recommendation 3.1 laparoscopic appendectomy on the next available list within 24 hours for uncomplicated appendicitis.

Airway assessment cut-offs as used in open-access airway studies (Europe PMC full texts read 10 October 2026, for example PMC13221751 and PMC13278463): thyromental distance 6.5 cm (6 to 6.5 cm borderline, below 6 cm high risk), inter-incisor gap 3 cm, neck movement graded by degrees (full, 80 to 90, below 80), Mallampati III or IV.

JBDS The Hospital Management of Hypoglycaemia in Adults with Diabetes Mellitus (4th edition PDF read; the 2018 edition and the NHS Wales 2022 revision were also read or searched): severe hypoglycaemia, give IV glucose over 15 minutes as 75 ml of 20% glucose or 150 ml of 10% glucose (the 2022 revision gives 100 ml of 20% or 200 ml of 10%); mild hypoglycaemia 15-20 g quick-acting carbohydrate; 10% or 20% glucose preferred over 50% because of extravasation injury.
UK Kidney Association Emergency Management of Hyperkalaemia in Adults hospital algorithm (Appendix 6, 2022 version, PDF read): insulin-glucose given in the first 15-30 minutes of the emergency treatment pathway; July 2026 guideline: 10 units soluble insulin in 25 g glucose, then 10% glucose 50 ml/hour for 5 hours if pre-treatment glucose is below 7 mmol/l.
Perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis in laparoscopic cholecystectomy (narrative review, PMC12916073, read): patient risk factors named in the literature are diabetes, obesity, age over 70, immunosuppression, prolonged operative time over 90 minutes and contamination; the review states that evidence for high-risk subgroups is insufficient for definite conclusions, so practice varies.
